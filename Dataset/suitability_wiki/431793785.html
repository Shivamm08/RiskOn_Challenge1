Fixed Income – Complex Bonds

 

What are the characteristics of a CoCo bond?


  • CoCo bonds are hybrid debt-equity instruments that have a conversion or principal write-down (or loss absorption) feature upon the occurrence of a trigger event.
  • The conversion or the write-down is triggered when e.g., the issuer is deemed non-viable by the regulator, or if it breaches the trigger level of the capital ratio, or if it breaches a trigger defined in the prospectus.


What are the main risks of CoCo bonds compared to normal bonds?


  • The risk of a total loss is much higher when investing into a normal bond of the same company because a write-off or conversion can be triggered even though the issuer is not in default.


Real Estate and Real Estate Investment Trusts

 

How can you invest into Real Estate?


  • Directly by purchasing a residential, commercial or industrial building, a rental property or land.
  • Indirectly via a real estate fund or a real estate investment trust (REIT).


What are the main risks when investing indirectly into Real Estate?


  • Interest rates: in case leverage (mortgage) is used, an interest rate increase has a negative impact on the valuation.
  • Liquidity: money invested in real estate is less liquid compared to equity or bond investments.
  • Regulatory risks: increase in taxation or other regulatory requirements has a negative impact on the valuation.
  • If investing indirectly, the specific risks of the vehicle come on top.


Non-Traditional Funds

 

What are hedge funds / alternative investment funds?


  • Hedge funds are actively managed investments schemes. Specifically, they are unconstrained investment funds that target absolute returns (or ‘alpha’) and employing non-traditional investment strategies.
  • Compared to traditional funds/mutual funds, they have access to an extensive toolbox of instruments (incl. derivatives or structured products) and techniques (such as short selling and leveraging).
  • Investing into hedge funds can be done directly or via a fund-of-hedge fund which invests in selected hedge funds – this leads to even less transparency, higher costs but a better diversification.


What are possible hedge fund strategies?


  • Relative value, event-driven, equity long/short, global macro, commodity trading advisors (CTA), (sorted from lower volatility and expected        return to higher volatility and expected return)


What are the main risks when investing into hedge funds? (Especially compared to traditional funds)


  • Volatility: much higher volatility due to the possibility to investing in derivatives, shorting positions, and leveraging.
  • Liquidity: very illiquid and money can often only be received back at certain points in time with long notice period.
  • Regulation: few to no regulation applies.
  • Transparency/price uncertainty: hedge fund prices are often published only occasionally.
  • Costs: Hedge funds often charge a performance fee.


Private Equity and Private Equity Funds

 

What are private equity investments?


  • Private equity is participation capital in companies that are not listed on a stock market (hence ‘private’, not public).
  • Participation ranges from early-stage financing (venture capital) to management buyouts (buyout capital).


How can you invest into PE?


  • Direct investment (rare case for single investors), via a private equity fund or via a private equity fund of fund.

 

What are the main risks when investing into private equity?


  • Liquidity: very illiquid, and long investment horizon, possibly without the option of early termination or sale.
  • Regulation: few to no regulation applies.
  • Transparency/price uncertainty: valuation of private equity (direct investments and funds) is very difficult.
  • Volatility: aim at absolute returns independently of the market environment.

 

Non-principal protected structured products (Yield Enhancement, Participation)

 

What are examples of yield enhancement/optimization and participation products in which you would invest and how do they work?


  • SPs are synthetic investment products that combine two or more instruments, one of them often being an option.
  • Yield enhancement products such as discount certificates or (barrier) reverse convertibles combine a fixed income product (a bond) with an option. This allows higher returns but causes additional risks (issuer risks, volatility, conversion to equities).
  • Participation products contain tracker certificates, outperformance certificates or bonus certificates.
  • Structured products expose the investor to one underlying instrument or a basket of underlying instruments which could be equities, commodities, fixed income, foreign exchange rates.

 

What are the main risks when investing into non-principal protected structured products?


  • Issuer risk – in case the issuer of the product defaults
  • Pricing risks – prices of products containing options do not change in a linear way
  • Underlying specific risks – volatility of the underlying instrument
  • Currency risk – if the product’s currency differs from the currency of the underlying instruments
  • Liquidity – not all products have a secondary market and often should be held until maturity
  • Tax risk – different payoffs are subject to different taxation
  • Pay-off specific risks – depending on the structured products type

 

Leveraged structured products

 

What are examples of leveraged products/certificates in which you would invest and how do they work?


  • These are products that make use of leverage effects to maximize possible returns.
  • Main category of leveraged structured products are Warrants which are securitized options.
  • Warrants expose the investor to one underlying instrument or a basket of underlying instruments which could be equities, commodities, fixed income, foreign exchange rates

 

What are the main risks when investing into leveraged products/certificates?


  • Potential for total loss or even higher loss than invested capital
  • Issuer risk – in case the issuer of the product defaults.
  • Pricing risks – prices of products containing options do not change in a linear way – the closer the maturity comes, the more dynamic the price movers.
  • Underlying specific risks – volatility of the underlying instrument.
  • Currency risk – if the product’s currency differs from the currency of the underlying instruments.

 

Exchange Traded Derivatives (ETD)

What are examples of ETDs in which you would invest and how do they work?


Traded Options:

  • By buying a call (put) option the investor has the right to buy (sell) an asset at a specific date and price.
  • A decision on whether the option right is exercised is done at maturity by the buyer of the option.
  • The value of an option depends on various factors (underlying price and its volatility, time to expiration).

Futures:

  • A future is a contractual obligation of the buyer to buy or sell an asset at a pre-determined price in the future.
  • Futures are highly standardized contracts for which a liquid market exists.
  • Futures trades involve ‘margining’, which allows a large exposure with a comparably low initial investment.


What are the main risks when investing into traded options and futures?

  • Potential losses if the client buys (long) a call or a put option are limited to the option premium.
  • Potential losses if the client sells (short) a call option are theoretically unlimited if the sale is uncovered.
  • ETD prices do not respond in a linear way to price movements of the underlying. Small changes of the underlying cause large variations the option’s/future’s price. The closer the expiry date, the lower the time value of the option.
  • Market risk of the underlying asset is directly reflected in the price and pay-off of the future.


OTC Derivatives

 

What is the difference between OTC Derivatives and ETD?


  • OTC derivatives are not standardized agreements between two parties.
  • An OTC derivative position cannot be sold until maturity, but a countertrade needs to be done.


What are the specific risks of OTC Derivatives on top of ETDs?


  • The two main additional risks of OTC Derivatives compared to ETD are counterparty/credit risk and liquidity risk.
  • Counterparty risk (risk that the counterparty defaults), liquidity risk (risk that the contract cannot be closed).


Accumulator / Decumulator / Target Accrual Redemption Forwards (TARFs)

 

How do accumulators, decumulators and TARFs work?


  • Investor is obliged to buy (AC) / sell (DC) a predefined underlying at strike price during the product’s lifetime.
  • The accumulation/decumulation stops as soon as a knock-out barrier is surpassed or when maturity is reached.
  • Settlement occurs on predefined regular intervals, e.g. daily, weekly or monthly.
  • The major difference between AC/DC and TARFs is that the KO is not depending on a specific spot price level, but on an accrued profit        specified at trade inception.


What are the specific risks of accumulator, decumulator and TARFs?


  • The product is traded on margin. Margin requirements are substantial and can fluctuate strongly.
  • Full downside risk. If the underlying falls below strike, the investor is obliged to pay at strike.
  • Products are OTC, hence there is a counterparty risk and liquidity risk.
  • Products can be leveraged (factor of 2 or 3) which increases the downside even further.


Commodities

What are commodities and in which goods do you want to be exposed?


  • Commodities are goods/material which must be sufficiently standardized
  • Examples are energy (crude oil, gas), base metals (copper), precious metals (gold), agricultural goods (wheat), livestock (cattle) or bulks (coal, iron ore).


How can you invest into Commodities?


  • Directly via precious metal accounts or physical entities (bars, coins).
  • Indirectly via futures, forwards, options, structured products or funds.


What are the main risks when investing into commodities?


  • Commodity prices are highly volatile and reason for price changes not always clear to investors.
  • Commodity prices respond differently to the economic cycle (impacted by natural, (geo)-political, country-specific and economic factors).
  • If investing indirectly, the specific risks of the vehicle come on top.

 

Digital Assets

 

What are digital assets?


  • Digital assets are assets based directly or indirectly on a blockchain or another digital, distributed, and encryption-based ledger or similar technology. Digital assets are neither supervised nor controlled by a central authority.


How can you invest into Digital Assets?


  • Directly by purchasing coins or tokens
  • Indirectly via funds, structured products or derivatives


What is hot storage? What is cold storage?


  • Hot: online storage – permanently connected to the internet (fast availability, less security)
  • Cold: offline storage – only connected to the internet for transfer of assets


What are the main risks when investing into digital assets?


  • Volatility: sudden, unexplainable and big price changes are not unusual
  • Liquidity: most of the market places are not/insufficiently regulated
  • Costs: comparably higher custody and brokerage costs
  •  Risk of losing access to digital assets
  •  Risk of unauthorized access, hacking or other sorts of cybercrime
  • If investing indirectly, the specific risks of the vehicle come on top
